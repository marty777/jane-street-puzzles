# Jane Street April 2026 Puzzle

## Description

The [April 2026 puzzle](https://www.janestreet.com/puzzles/can-u-dig-it-index/), titled "Can U Dig It?", presents a 14-by-14 grid of characters, along with a note that the authors have "drawn a blank" with the puzzle's instructions and that the answer is a postive integer.

## Solution

The puzzle has the appearance of a [word search](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Word_search), and several linear English words are visible in horizontal, vertical or diagonal orientations. However, the puzzle is also asking, in a roundabout way through the title, for concave "U" shaped arrangements of letters that spell out words. The U-shaped words are on the general theme of "digit" (dig it), and the linear words are on the general theme of "can", with the U-shaped and linear words appearing in distinctively joined pairs.

## Discussion

The above fits together neatly, but getting from the initial puzzle as presented to the solution required several non-obvious logical leaps for me. It's worth noting that I took a risk and submitted an answer before having fully solved the puzzle, although I was pretty sure that I'd inferred it correctly.

1. The general appearance of the grid and several visible linear words suggested this was a word search puzzle. Perhaps finding words in the grid would reveal instructions for producing the answer? The prominent `find` in the top row and the only hyphen on the grid appearing on the bottom row seemed possibly important, and potentially the bottom row might be a garbled form of `twenty-six`, plausible because JS puzzles sometimes reference the current year.
2. Beginning from `find` in the top row it's possible to make the opening of a sentence, `findthestart`, if you follow a non-linear path between neighboring cells in the top two lines on the grid, although there doesn't seem to be a way to continue the phrase. Maybe a meandering path through the grid would reference the journey in [*The Warriors*](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bTUrWYv2vtU) somehow? To look for other possible words or phrases that could be made from arbitrary paths rather than linear ones in the word search, I grabbed a [dictionary file of (mostly) English words](https://github.com/dwyl/english-words) and set up a method to find examples.
3. It turns out you can find thousands of strings matching words from my dictionary file in this grid if you follow arbitrary paths, especially since the file contains many unlikely possibilities like acronyms and abbreviations, place names, alternate spellings, etc. Most matches were very short and obviously not useful. Filtering for longer words, several popped out including `hexadecimal` and `appendage` and `aluminum`.
4. Reviewing the longest single-word matches plotted on the grid, I eventually noticed that some were forming symmetrical concave shapes and tied this to the "U" clue in the puzzle title.
5. Thinking that only U-shaped words were part of the solution, I stopped looking at linear words and set up a method to find single dictionary words in the grid with concave shapes. This worked fairly well, but there are still a surprising number of words that can be formed from concave shapes (mostly short and asymmetric ones). Sorting through them, I found `integer`, `binary` and `hundreds` as examples that were both symmetrically concave and plausible words that might relate to a positive integer solution to the puzzle. I also found a number of words that didn't obviously relate to an integer solution, like `hallux` (which I'm glad I looked up) `pinky` and `thumb`. I had the idea that some of these might indicate masks for digits of a number in a particular base (e.g. `thumb` for the first digit, `pinky` for the fifth). I didn't spot the theme and wordplay on "digit" until later. There were also a number of promising but ultimately non-applicable words like `ionise`, `laminarite`, `amine`, `argon`, `guest`, `ninja`, `pared`, `solum` and `themed` that were symmetrically U-shaped and couldn't be immediately ruled out.
6. I got stuck at this point, because I couldn't spot a way to tie these words together into instructions. I tried testing for candidate words that might start or end neighboring other candidates I'd found, thinking a path constituting an entire phrase might still be found in the grid, but without much luck (and a lot of false positives).
7. Putting the problem aside for a while, I came up with the idea that the U-words might be arranged as a diagram, with the position and the orientation of the shapes indicating a sequence of operations. `hexadecimal` pointing away from `hundreds` might mean taking some value, extracting the digit in the hundreds column, and then expressing it in hexadecimal, or something like that.
8. That didn't pan out, but it made me take a look at the grid with fresh eyes. I first noticed `opener` was next to `hundreds` and spotted that several of the U-shaped words I'd found were paired with linear words in the same way.
9. Setting up a method to search for paired U-shaped and linear English words, I found several more and was able to discard some of the previous U-shaped candidates. I guessed that the common theme of the U-shaped words was something like "digits", but the linear words seemed random. The pairs included some spurious extras like `anno` paired with `gain` in the top-right quadrant, `haiti` paired with `dinar` in the bottom-left, and `erase` paired with `pop` in the bottom-right. `anno` could plausibly have something to do with digits, while `haiti` and `erase` seemed more of a stretch but couldn't be ruled out.
10. I finally figured out that the common theme of the linear words was "can", and tied that back to the puzzle title. That seemed to rule out `gain` and `dinar`, which didn't seem related to "can", but I couldn't be sure. In retrospect the `anno`/`gain` pair doesn't work because every other example pairing has the first and last characters of both words as neighbors, while `gain` and `anno` are the wrong way round.
11. With the words found so far, it seemed worth trying a possibility suggested by the conspicuous "drawing a blank" phrase in the puzzle instructions and deleting cells on the grid that either were or weren't part of the found words. I also experimented with removing the interiors of the closed areas formed by paired words and including/omitting various word pairs. My best result looked like `ifindthretgnetawigeyertnmethsmalflestnumbeerwithdeigigtsttoetalitiangnibtewenty-tsix` when word pairs were removed from the grid, the remaining characters were read in raster order, and with `haiti`/`dinar` removed from the word list. The result is garbled but pretty obviously contains the phrase "find...smallest number with digits totalling twenty-six".
12. Plotting the grid cells which would need to be removed to match the legible parts of the phrase, I quickly found `baseten` paired with `paint`. I also could mark out other cells on a the grid which must be part of words, even if the shapes that would produce those weren't easy to spot. 
13. At that point, I was stuck. I couldn't find any other words on the grid and "...smallest number with digits totalling twenty-six" (which is 899 if a postive integer) wasn't a complete enough phrase to be sure it was the correct answer. I put the problem aside.
14. After a break, I came back to try attacking the most illegible part of the instruction phrase directly, as well as experiment with different combinations of word pairs in case some were false matches. I set up a method to look for potential English words that could be formed by deleting characters in the instruction string. With `anno`/`gain` removed from the word list, `ifindthretgnetaowigeyenrtnmieth` contains quite a few matches, including "one", "three", "the", "not", "omit", many short unlikely ones, and most significantly "twentieth". It required a large number of characters to be removed, but the phrase "find the twentieth smallest number with digits totalling twenty-six" seemed compellingly likely to be right based on previous JS puzzles that reference the current year.
15. With a list of cells on the grid that must be part of words to produce the probable instruction phrase, I quickly found `ringtoe` and an irregularly U-shaped `finger` in the top-left quadrant which I'd missed in searches because it wasn't a symmetrical concave shape. This didn't cover all the cells that should be removed to produce a clear instruction phrase, and `finger` being asymmetric  (and unpaired) seemed odd, but it was encouraging enough that I knew I had the instructions properly figured out. I calculated the corresponding integer and submitted. The answer, fortunately, was accepted as correct. Even if I couldn't finish the puzzle, I would at least get to see the complete list of words when the solution was posted. 
16. Taking a look a few days later, I finally realised out that "dig it" in the puzzle title punned on "digit". Confirming the theme, the `erase`/`pop` pair bothered me, since `erase` didn't seem to fit with the pattern even if `pop` made sense for the "can" theme. Taking a closer look, I noticed `pointer` extending from one of the "p"s in `pop` to join with `finger`, making the symmetric U-shaped `pointerfinger`. This was exciting, but the long diagonal that would pair with the word didn't make any sense. I looked for ways to break it up into something comprehensible and googled substrings for a while before finally paying attention to the results for the full string `egebamyasi`. I was very amused once I realised what I was looking at. With that pair included, the instruction phrase is completely clear and readible.

Overall, I spend maybe 6 hours playing around with this over a few afternoons. Very enjoyable and I'm glad I was able to solve it fully eventually.
